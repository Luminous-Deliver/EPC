The Minimum Energy Efficiency Standards (MEES) prohibit letting residential properties in England and Wales rated below EPC band E. Since April 2020, this rule applies to all private tenancies — not just new ones. Fines reach £30,000 per property. A proposed upgrade to band C by 2028–2030 is under active consultation.

## What MEES currently requires (2026)

The rules are straightforward: you cannot lawfully let a residential property in England or Wales with an EPC rating below E. "Letting" includes granting a new tenancy, renewing an existing tenancy, and continuing an existing periodic tenancy.

The current standard has been in force since:
- **1 April 2018** — new tenancies and renewals
- **1 April 2020** — all tenancies, including existing periodic agreements

This means that if you have a long-standing tenant who moved in before 2018, MEES has still applied to that tenancy since April 2020. A property rated F or G cannot be legally continued as a let, regardless of tenancy age.

### What triggers a MEES obligation?

You need a valid EPC, and that EPC must show E or above, when:
- Granting a new assured shorthold tenancy (AST)
- Renewing a fixed-term tenancy
- A fixed-term tenancy rolls into a statutory periodic tenancy (this is treated as a new tenancy for MEES purposes — an area of frequent confusion)
- Marketing the property for rent (the EPC must be available to prospective tenants before viewing)

Short lets of under 4 months, agricultural tenancies, and some listed buildings may be exempt — see below.

## Penalties for non-compliance

Enforcement is handled by local authorities (usually the private sector housing team). The penalty structure is:

| Breach Duration | Maximum Fine |
|----------------|-------------|
| Under 3 months | £5,000 |
| 3 months or longer | £30,000 |
| Providing false information to the exemption register | £5,000 |

Fines are per property, not per landlord. A portfolio landlord with five F-rated flats faces up to £150,000 in fines if all are let in breach. Details of enforcement actions are published on a publicly accessible database, which is searchable by local authority — reputational risk is real.

In London, enforcement activity varies significantly by borough. Tower Hamlets, Newham, and Hackney have run active campaigns targeting below-E properties in the private rented sector. Outer-London boroughs have generally been less active, though this is changing as councils face housing quality targets.

## Exemptions: what qualifies and how to register

Exemptions are narrow and temporary. They must be registered on the **PRS Exemptions Register** (gov.uk) before you continue letting the property. An unregistered exemption is not valid — this is a common mistake that results in fines.

### Valid exemption categories

**Cost cap exemption:** All relevant improvements would cost more than £3,500 (including VAT). "Relevant improvements" are those recommended on the EPC and capable of improving the rating. You must get quotes from at least three installers, carry out all improvements that cost under £3,500 in total, and register the exemption with evidence.

**Consent exemption:** A third party — tenant, lender, or planning authority — has refused consent for the required works. The refusal must be in writing and reasonable attempts to secure consent must be documented.

**Devaluation exemption:** An independent surveyor has confirmed in writing that carrying out the required improvements would reduce the property's market value by more than 5%.

**Property exemption:** The property is genuinely incapable of improvement — typically very unusual circumstances. This is not a catch-all.

**New landlord exemption:** You recently became landlord through inheritance or repossession. This lasts six months.

All exemptions last five years, with the exception of the new landlord exemption (six months) and the consent exemption (five years, or until the tenant changes, whichever comes first). After expiry, you must reassess and either improve the property or re-register.

## The proposed upgrade to band C

The UK government's 2023 consultation on raising MEES to EPC band C proposed:
- **2028**: minimum band C for new tenancies
- **2030**: minimum band C for all tenancies (including existing)

At the time of writing in May 2026, the legislation has not been passed. However, the direction of policy is clear and has been consistent across governments. Landlords should plan for these dates.

The cost cap for the upgraded standard is expected to be £15,000 per property (up from the current £3,500). This matters because many solid-wall London properties currently qualify for a cost-cap exemption at the E standard — the higher cap may close that exemption for future C compliance.

## Common scenarios and what to do

### Scenario: You have an F-rated property with a current tenant

You are likely in breach of MEES. You cannot simply wait for the tenancy to end — MEES applies to ongoing periodic tenancies. Options:

1. Improve the property to E or above (required unless an exemption applies)
2. Check whether all relevant improvements would cost over £3,500 and register a cost-cap exemption with evidence
3. Check whether the property qualifies for another exemption

Do not issue a section 21 notice to evict the tenant as a mechanism to avoid improvement costs — this is widely regarded as retaliatory eviction and is factually pointless since you'd still need to improve the property before re-letting.

### Scenario: You have a G-rated Victorian terrace in Islington

Victorian solid-wall properties in London are the MEES problem cases. With no cavity to fill, the main routes to E are:
- Modern heating controls and boiler (if the boiler is old)
- LED lighting
- Loft insulation (if accessible)
- Draught-proofing

These may collectively get a G to E or even D. If the total cost of all recommended measures exceeds £3,500, a cost-cap exemption applies — but you must document this properly.

### Scenario: You're buying an F-rated property at auction

MEES applies from the moment you become the landlord. You have six months under the new landlord exemption, but you should factor improvement costs into your purchase calculation. An F-rated London terrace with unimproved solid walls may need £8,000–£16,000 to reach E reliably.

## What to do now

1. **Check every property's EPC rating** — on the government EPC register at find-energy-certificate.service.gov.uk (free, search by postcode)
2. **Commission a new EPC for any property with an expired certificate** (EPCs last 10 years) — see our [EPC validity guide](/blog/how-long-does-epc-last)
3. **For sub-E properties, get improvement quotes** — cavity wall insulation, loft insulation, and boiler upgrades are the highest-impact measures for most properties
4. **Check ECO4 eligibility** — if your tenant receives qualifying benefits, insulation and heating upgrades may be fully funded
5. **Register any genuine exemptions** before continuing to let

## How enforcement actually works

Local authority housing officers typically identify non-compliant properties through:
- Proactive data-matching (EPC register vs. tenancy deposit and letting agent records)
- Tenant complaints
- Referrals from other council departments (housing benefit, council tax)

If contacted by your local authority, respond promptly and provide evidence of either compliance, exemption registration, or an improvement plan. Councils generally prefer compliance over fines, and a documented improvement timeline — even if not yet complete — is treated more favourably than silence.
