During an EPC assessment, an accredited assessor visits your property and records around 40 data points — covering walls, roof, floor, glazing, heating system, hot water, and lighting. The visit takes 30 minutes for a studio flat and up to 2 hours for a large detached house. The rating is generated by RdSAP 10 software after the visit, not estimated on-site.

## What the assessor actually looks at

The EPC is built from a systematic survey of your property's physical characteristics. Here's what we record at each stage of the visit.

### 1. External inspection and dimensions

The assessor starts outside (or uses floor plan dimensions where available) to record:
- **Floor area** — measured room by room. This is the total heated floor area, excluding unheated spaces like garages.
- **Construction age** — the age band (pre-1900, 1900–1929, 1930–1949, etc.) determines the assumed U-values for walls and floors if they can't be directly measured.
- **Property type** — detached, semi-detached, end-terrace, mid-terrace, or flat (including floor position). Flats on upper floors have fewer exposed surfaces and typically score better for fabric heat loss.

### 2. Walls

The assessor identifies wall construction type and any insulation:
- **Solid walls** — single-leaf brick, stone, or concrete. Thickness is measured at a window reveal. Typical pre-1919 London terrace: 9-inch solid brick.
- **Cavity walls** — identified by reveal width or drill test in some cases. The assessor records whether cavity insulation is present (confirmed by a BBA certificate, building control record, or visual evidence such as drill holes).
- **External wall insulation (EWI)** — visually identifiable from rendered insulation on the outside. Depth and type recorded if accessible.

If wall insulation cannot be confirmed, the software defaults to uninsulated — which is the most conservative (lowest) assumption for SAP calculation.

### 3. Roof and loft

- **Loft insulation depth** — measured directly with a probe or ruler if the loft is accessible. The standard reference depths are 0mm (no insulation), 25mm, 50mm, 75mm, 100mm, 150mm, 200mm, and 270mm+.
- **Roof type** — pitched, flat, or room-in-roof (where the habitable space extends into the roof). Room-in-roof construction uses different U-value assumptions.
- **If the loft is inaccessible** — the assessor defaults to a regional age-band assumption, which is typically uninsulated or poorly insulated for pre-1980s properties.

### 4. Ground floor

The floor construction type and any insulation are recorded:
- Suspended timber floor, solid concrete, or insulated slab
- Underfloor insulation (if confirmed)

Assessors cannot lift carpet or flooring, so floor insulation is often defaulted unless building records confirm it. This particularly affects properties with suspended timber floors, which RdSAP assumes are uninsulated unless evidence exists.

### 5. Windows and doors

- **Glazing type** — single, double, or triple. For double glazing, the approximate installation date determines the assumed U-value (older double glazing has a higher heat loss than modern units).
- **Frame material** — uPVC, wood, aluminium. Affects assumed U-values.
- **Proportion of glazing** — estimated as a percentage of wall area.

New double glazing (installed post-2002) is treated as full double glazing with a standard U-value. Replacing double glazing with newer double glazing adds no SAP points in most cases.

### 6. Heating system

This is one of the most heavily weighted factors in the SAP calculation:
- **Main heating fuel** — gas, electricity, oil, heat pump, district heating
- **Main heating system type** — combi boiler, system boiler, storage heaters, heat pump
- **Boiler efficiency** — modern condensing boilers (post-2005) have an assumed efficiency of around 89–92%. Pre-2005 non-condensing boilers: 70–80%.
- **Heating controls** — room thermostat, programmer, thermostatic radiator valves (TRVs), weather compensation. Each control type adds points.
- **Secondary heating** — gas fires, electric panel heaters used as backup

### 7. Hot water

- **How hot water is generated** — combi boiler, separate cylinder with immersion, solar thermal, heat pump
- **Hot water cylinder** — size, insulation jacket thickness (if applicable)
- **Immersion heater** — single or dual element

Electric immersion heaters score very poorly in RdSAP. A combi boiler with no cylinder scores much better for hot water.

### 8. Renewable energy

- **Solar PV** — system size (kWp), orientation, pitch, any shading
- **Solar thermal** — collector size, orientation
- **Wind turbines** — on-site generation

Solar PV is the highest-impact renewable on the SAP calculation. A south-facing 3kWp system with no shading is credited fully; a north-facing or heavily shaded system gets reduced credit.

### 9. Lighting

The assessor counts the number of fixed light fittings and how many are low-energy (LED or compact fluorescent). Since October 2025 under RdSAP 10, all low-energy fittings are assumed to be LED (the previous standard distinguished between LED and CFL). A property where all fittings are LED gets maximum lighting credit.

## How long does the assessment take?

| Property type | Typical duration |
|--------------|-----------------|
| Studio or 1-bedroom flat | 30–45 minutes |
| 2-bedroom flat or house | 45–60 minutes |
| 3-bedroom house | 60–90 minutes |
| 4-bedroom house | 75–90 minutes |
| 5+ bedroom house | 90–120 minutes |

These are typical times for a well-prepared property with good access. A property with a difficult loft access, missing documentation for the heating system, or multiple outbuildings takes longer.

## What to have ready before the assessor arrives

You don't need to prepare your property in any cosmetic way. The assessor is measuring physical characteristics, not appearance. However, access and documentation can speed up the visit and improve accuracy:

- **Access to all rooms** including any room-in-roof spaces, utility rooms, and conservatories
- **Access to the loft** — clear the hatch if possible; the assessor needs to measure insulation depth
- **Access to the boiler** — the model and efficiency rating are recorded; the make, model, and installation date are on the boiler casing
- **Any existing building records** — FENSA certificates for windows, BBA certificates for wall insulation, building control sign-off for floor insulation. These allow the assessor to override default assumptions with confirmed data, often improving the rating.
- **Meter type** — smart meter, Economy 7 (two-rate), or standard single-rate

## Misconceptions about EPC assessments

**"The assessor can see what rating I'll get before the visit."** No — the rating comes from RdSAP 10 software processing the data after the visit. The assessor can estimate based on experience, but the software produces the official score.

**"I should tidy up or redecorate before the assessment."** Tidying helps with access but doesn't affect the rating. The assessor isn't grading aesthetics.

**"The rating reflects my energy bills."** It doesn't. The EPC is a standardised model using fixed occupancy and temperature assumptions. Your actual bills depend on your behaviour.

**"I can't improve my rating without spending a lot."** The first improvements (LEDs, loft insulation) are often cheap and impactful. See our [EPC improvement guide](/blog/how-to-improve-epc-rating) for a full ranked list.

## After the assessment

The assessor enters the data into Elmhurst's accredited RdSAP 10 software, which calculates the SAP score and generates the certificate. The certificate is then lodged on the government EPC register and issued to you.

The certificate is valid for **ten years** from the date of issue. You can commission a new assessment at any time if you've made improvements — the newer certificate replaces the old one on the register.
