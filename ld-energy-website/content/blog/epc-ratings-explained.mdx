An EPC rating is a score between 1 and 100 Standard Assessment Procedure (SAP) points, mapped to a letter band from A (most efficient, 92+) to G (least efficient, 1–20). The rating reflects a property's modelled energy efficiency based on construction, insulation, heating, and hot water systems — not actual energy bills. In England and Wales, an EPC is legally required when a property is sold or rented, and the certificate is valid for ten years.

## What does each EPC band mean?

The UK uses a fixed scale to convert SAP points into letter bands. The same scale applies whether you live in a Hackney terrace, a Bromley semi, or a Westminster mansion flat — the score is normalised by floor area so that small and large homes can be compared fairly.

| Band | SAP Points | Typical Property |
|------|-----------|------------------|
| A | 92–100 | New-build with heat pump and solar PV |
| B | 81–91 | Recent new-build, well-insulated, modern boiler |
| C | 69–80 | Modern home or older property after improvements |
| D | 55–68 | Average UK home — most pre-2000 properties |
| E | 39–54 | Older home with limited insulation, ageing heating |
| F | 21–38 | Solid-wall pre-war property, old heating, single glazing |
| G | 1–20 | Severely inefficient — rare in the rental market post-MEES |

The scale is non-linear in practice: moving from G to E is usually faster and cheaper than moving from D to C, because the easy wins (insulation, lighting, basic heating controls) get exhausted quickly. We see this constantly in the field — a 1930s solid-wall semi can leap from F to D with cavity-style external wall insulation, but the same house may need a heat pump or solar PV to push past C.

## How is the rating actually calculated?

A Domestic Energy Assessor (DEA) visits the property and records around 40 data points: external wall construction, insulation type and depth, roof construction, floor type, window glazing and frames, heating system make and model, hot water cylinder size and insulation, ventilation, and lighting count. Since October 2025, all new assessments must use **RdSAP 10**, the updated Reduced data Standard Assessment Procedure methodology — replacing the previous RdSAP 9.94 standard that ran from 2012.

The data is entered into accredited software (we use Elmhurst's tools as Elmhurst-accredited assessors), which calculates the SAP score against fixed assumptions about occupancy, internal temperatures, and UK climate. The result is a rating that reflects the property itself, not how the current occupants happen to use it. A frugal household running a G-rated home will still get a G; a heavy energy user in an A-rated flat will still get an A.

This is worth understanding because owners are often surprised when their actual bills don't match the EPC's predicted costs. The EPC isn't measuring your behaviour — it's measuring the building.

## Where do most London homes sit on the scale?

London's housing stock skews older than the national average, which pushes ratings down. A few patterns we see across the capital:

- **Victorian and Edwardian terraces** (very common across Hackney, Islington, Lambeth, Wandsworth) typically rate D or E. Solid 9-inch brick walls, original sash windows, and limited loft access make C rating challenging without significant intervention.
- **Post-war estate flats** (Tower Hamlets, Newham, Lambeth) often rate D or C, with newer purpose-built blocks reaching B.
- **1930s semis and inter-war suburbs** (Ealing, Croydon, Bromley, Enfield) typically rate D, with cavity walls offering an easier path to C than Victorian solid-wall housing.
- **New-builds since 2010** generally rate B, occasionally A where heat pumps and solar PV are fitted from the start.

Across England as a whole, the most recent government statistics show around 60% of homes in bands D and E, with band D the single most common rating. That's roughly 13 million homes nationally, and the picture in inner London is very similar.

## How does the rating affect selling and renting?

For sellers, the EPC must be commissioned before marketing. Estate agents cannot legally list a property for sale without one, and the rating appears on every listing portal — Rightmove, Zoopla, OnTheMarket. Several studies, including 2013 research commissioned by DECC and a 2020 analysis from Nationwide, found a measurable correlation between higher EPC bands and sale-price premiums, generally in the 1–6% range. The effect is strongest at the extremes: B-rated homes command a clear premium over F- or G-rated comparables.

If you're preparing to sell in London, see our practical guide on the [EPC requirements for selling your home](/blog/epc-for-selling-house) — there's a small but real opportunity to nudge the rating up before listing.

For landlords, the situation is more pressing. Under the **Minimum Energy Efficiency Standards (MEES)**, every rental property in England and Wales must currently have a valid EPC rated **E or above**. Letting below E is a civil offence with fines up to £5,000 per property. The government has formally proposed raising the minimum to **C by 2028 for new tenancies and C by 2030 for all tenancies**, with consultations under way at the time of writing. We cover the full landlord picture in our [MEES regulations guide](/blog/mees-regulations-2026) and our [EPC C by 2030 timeline](/blog/epc-c-by-2030).

## How do I find my property's current EPC?

Every EPC issued since 2008 is on the public **gov.uk EPC register**. Search by postcode at [find-energy-certificate.service.gov.uk](https://find-energy-certificate.service.gov.uk) — the certificate is free to view and download. If your property has never been assessed, or the existing EPC is older than 10 years, you'll need a new one before sale or new tenancy.

A common surprise: the EPC register is property-specific, not owner-specific. The previous owner's EPC, if still within the 10-year validity window, is still legally usable for marketing — but it may not reflect any improvements (or deteriorations) since it was issued. If you've added insulation, replaced a boiler, or fitted solar PV, a fresh assessment will usually pay for itself by lifting the rating.

## What rating should you aim for?

The honest answer depends on what you're doing with the property:

- **Selling soon, with budget for one or two improvements**: aim for the next band up. Moving D to C, or E to D, has a measurable impact on listing perception and gives buyers' surveyors less to flag.
- **Long-term landlord**: aim for **C** ahead of the proposed 2028/2030 deadlines. Doing it now, between tenancies, avoids the void-period rush in 2027–2030 when every other landlord is also trying to hit C.
- **Owner-occupier improving for comfort and bills**: aim for **B**. The jump from C to B usually means heating system changes (heat pump or hybrid) and is where energy bills genuinely fall.
- **New-build buyer**: anything below B is unusual; A is achievable with solar PV.

For most London properties built before 1990, **C is a realistic ceiling without significant retrofit spending**. Going to B usually requires either a heat pump or solar PV, both of which are more disruptive than the standard insulation-and-LED package that gets you to C.

## How can you improve your EPC rating?

The cheapest single improvement is almost always **switching all bulbs to LEDs** — typically £50–£150 for a 3-bed home and worth 1–3 SAP points. The highest-impact mid-budget improvement for most London properties is **loft insulation topped up to 270mm**, costing £400–£700 and worth 4–8 points. Cavity wall insulation, where suitable (most properties built between the 1920s and 1990s), adds another 5–10 points for £400–£1,500.

A working DEA can talk you through which recommendations are realistic for your property and which (often the heat pump suggestion on a small terrace) are unlikely to make sense.

## What happens if your rating is below E?

If you're a landlord, a property rating F or G cannot legally be let to a new tenant, and you have 28 days from notice to either improve it to E, register an exemption on the **PRS Exemptions Register**, or face enforcement. Exemptions exist for genuinely unimprovable properties (typically listed buildings or where all relevant improvements have been made up to a £3,500 cost cap), but they are time-limited and require evidence.

For owner-occupiers, there's no legal compulsion — but an F or G rating will affect resale and is a good signal that the property is costing significantly more to heat than it needs to. The single biggest win for an F or G is usually a heating system upgrade (replacing storage heaters or an old non-condensing boiler), often combined with a loft insulation top-up.
