Selling a property in England or Wales requires a valid EPC before marketing — not at exchange, not at completion, but before the first listing goes live. The certificate must be available to prospective buyers, and estate agents cannot legally market a property without one.

## The legal position for sellers

The Energy Performance of Buildings Regulations 2012 require that an EPC be commissioned and made available to buyers before a property is offered for sale. The specific requirements are:

- The EPC must be **commissioned before marketing begins** — before the property is listed on any portal or advertised in any medium
- Estate agents are required to include the EPC rating in all property advertisements (the coloured energy rating graphic on Rightmove, Zoopla, etc.)
- The full EPC certificate must be provided free of charge to any prospective buyer who requests it
- The EPC must be handed over to the buyer at completion

The penalty for a seller who fails to provide an EPC is a **£200 fixed penalty**. The estate agent also faces enforcement action for marketing without one. In practice, most agents will not take a property to market until they confirm a valid EPC is in place.

**Exception:** Standalone buildings with a total useful floor area of fewer than 50m² are exempt. Listed buildings are also generally exempt (see FAQs).

## When to commission the EPC

The right time is **before you formally instruct an estate agent and before any portal listing is prepared**. Many sellers get caught by not knowing this and assuming the EPC is something that happens during conveyancing.

A practical timeline for London sellers:

- **4–6 weeks before listing**: Decide on estate agent, prepare property for photography
- **3–4 weeks before listing**: Commission EPC assessment (takes 1–2 days for assessment + up to 72 hours for certificate)
- **2–3 weeks before listing**: EPC in hand; instruct agent to prepare listing materials including EPC rating
- **Day of listing**: Full marketing begins — EPC already lodged and available

If you're in a hurry, next-day EPC certificates are available for an additional charge. Most London sellers can have a certificate in hand within 48–72 hours of booking.

## Does EPC rating affect sale price?

The honest answer is: it correlates at the extremes, and the middle of the scale is harder to disentangle.

Several studies have looked at this question:

- A **2013 DECC (Department of Energy and Climate Change) study** found statistically significant price differences between EPC bands, with A/B-rated homes selling for around 5% more than D-rated comparables, and F/G-rated homes selling for around 3.5% less than D-rated comparables, after controlling for other factors.
- **2020 Nationwide research** found a premium of approximately 1.7% per EPC band improvement at the top of the scale, narrowing to statistically insignificant levels in the D–C range.
- **Zoopla analysis (2021)** found that homes rated A or B sold around 6% faster than D-rated equivalents in the same area.

The practical implications for London sellers:

**F or G rated properties:** There is a genuine market impact. Some mortgage lenders restrict lending on properties below EPC E, and buyers who intend to let the property cannot do so legally. This limits your buyer pool and creates downward price pressure. Improving from G to E before selling is almost always worth the investment.

**E-rated properties:** There is a measurable impact at the margins. Some first-time buyer mortgage products now screen for EPC ratings. Improvement to D is worth considering if achievable cheaply.

**D-rated properties:** The most common rating in London. Moving to C shows a modest effect in research — often cited as around 1–2% premium — but for a typical London property worth £500,000, that's £5,000–£10,000, which may justify spending £800–£2,000 on insulation.

**C-rated properties:** Selling well already. Unless the property is close to the B boundary and solar PV is feasible, further improvement is unlikely to pay back at point of sale.

## What rating should you aim for before listing?

This depends on your current rating and the cost of improvement:

### If you're currently D-rated

The cheapest improvements — LED lighting and loft insulation — often cost under £800 combined and can add 5–10 SAP points, potentially pushing a D into the high D or low C range. For a property at D65 or D66, this could be enough to reach C with minimal spend.

If loft insulation is already in place and lights are already LED, reaching C from D may require cavity wall insulation (£400–£1,500 for a suitable property) or a boiler upgrade (£2,000–£4,000 if the existing boiler is old).

**Our recommendation for D-rated sellers:** Commission the EPC first, get your exact SAP score, and ask your assessor which single measure would most cheaply lift you to C. If it's £800 or less, do it.

### If you're currently E-rated

Improving from E to D is more achievable than E to C. For buyers who intend to let the property, any improvement above E removes the MEES compliance concern. The cheapest improvements (LEDs, loft top-up, draught-proofing) typically add 4–8 points for £500–£900.

Whether to invest in reaching C from E depends on your property type. A cavity-wall 1970s flat may reach C for £1,500. A Victorian solid-wall terrace may need £10,000+ to make the jump reliably.

### If you're currently F or G-rated

Act before marketing. An F or G EPC creates a restricted buyer pool and active concerns for mortgage lenders. Even reaching E (the current minimum for letting) opens your market significantly. The cheapest route to E for most properties is heating controls, LED lighting, and loft insulation — often achievable for £500–£1,200.

## Getting the EPC before improvements vs. after

If you plan to make improvements before selling, you face a timing question: get the EPC before or after the works?

**Option A — Get EPC first, then improve, then re-assess**
This gives you the exact SAP score and shows you exactly which measures will push you to the target band. You can then commission a new EPC after the works to confirm the improved rating. The initial EPC costs £49–£79; the second assessment costs the same. Total spend: £100–£160 for two assessments. Worth it for clarity.

**Option B — Improve, then get EPC once**
If you're confident about the works (e.g., you're installing known-good loft insulation to a confirmed depth), you can do the works and get a single EPC afterward. Risk: if the works don't deliver the expected points, you need a third option.

For most sellers, Option A is lower-risk because it removes the guesswork.
