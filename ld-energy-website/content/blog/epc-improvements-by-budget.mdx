Improving an EPC rating does not have to mean a heat pump and a five-figure bill. Most homes climb the scale through a series of modest, well-chosen measures, and the right ones depend almost entirely on your budget and what the property already has.

This guide breaks the options into three budgets — roughly £500, £2,000 and £5,000 — and explains what each measure does and what decides how much it helps.

A quick reminder of the scale: an EPC score runs from 1 to 100 SAP points, band D starts at 55 and band C at 69. How far you have to travel depends on where your property currently sits, which is on your certificate.

## Under £500: the quick wins

If your budget is a few hundred pounds, focus on lighting, controls and heat retention. None of these require structural work, and most can be done in a weekend or by a handyman.

### LED lighting throughout

The best pound-for-pound improvement. The EPC records the proportion of fixed lighting outlets with low-energy bulbs, so swap every remaining halogen and incandescent for LEDs. The effect is largest in a house that still has mostly old bulbs — and negligible in one already fully converted.

### Hot water cylinder jacket

If you have a hot water cylinder with thin or no insulation, an 80mm jacket is one of the cheapest measures in existence. If your cylinder is already factory-insulated foam, or you have a combi boiler with no cylinder, this does not apply.

### Heating controls

RdSAP rewards a full set of controls: a programmer, a room thermostat and thermostatic radiator valves (TRVs). Many older systems are missing the room thermostat or TRVs. Adding the missing pieces is a small job for a heating engineer and reliably lifts the score.

### Draught proofing

Sealing gaps around windows, doors, letterboxes and floors reduces the modelled heat loss. The point gain is modest, but the comfort improvement is immediate and tenants notice it.

Spend the full £500 across these and a property starting in the low 60s can realistically pick up 4-8 points, sometimes enough to cross into C on its own.

## Under £2,000: insulation does the heavy lifting

With up to £2,000, you can do everything in the first tier and add one or both of the highest-impact insulation measures.

### Loft insulation top-up to 270mm

If your loft has less than 100mm of insulation, topping it up to the recommended 270mm is one of the biggest single gains available. The materials are cheap, the labour is straightforward, and the gain is largest for properties starting with little or no insulation. We cover the details in our guide to [loft insulation and EPC ratings](/blog/loft-insulation-epc-rating).

### Cavity wall insulation

Where the property has unfilled cavity walls, filling them is usually the largest gain in this price range. Most homes built between roughly 1930 and 1990 have cavities, and many remain unfilled. A borescope check confirms suitability, and exposure to driving rain needs assessing first. Our [cavity wall insulation guide](/blog/cavity-wall-insulation-epc) covers when it is and is not a good idea.

### Putting the tier together

A common London scenario, typical of the inter-war and 1950s semis across [Ealing](/areas/ealing), [Croydon](/areas/croydon) and [Enfield](/areas/enfield): a mid-terrace rated mid-D, with unfilled cavities, 100mm of loft insulation, partial LED lighting and no TRVs. Cavity fill plus a loft top-up plus the tier-one measures will typically move a property like this comfortably into band C. In our experience, many typical London D-rated homes can reach C for under £2,500 without touching the heating system at all.

## Under £5,000: heating and bigger projects

Above £2,000, the options shift to the heating system and the building fabric's harder problems.

### Replacing an old non-condensing boiler

If your boiler is an older non-condensing unit, replacing it with a modern condensing boiler is the standout measure in this tier, because heating efficiency is a major input to the calculation and heating usually dominates a home's energy use. If your boiler is already a condensing model, replacing it changes little — spend the money elsewhere.

### Solar PV, partial install

A smaller solar array fits this budget and helps by generating electricity on site. How much depends on roof orientation, shading and array size. Solar is also one of the few measures that keeps helping beyond band C, which matters if you are aiming for B.

### Secondary glazing

Where full double glazing is impractical or not permitted, for example in conservation areas, secondary glazing improves the modelled window performance and cuts draughts and noise. The effect on the rating is usually modest, so treat it as a comfort and compliance measure rather than a route to a new band.

## Summary table

Measures roughly cheapest-first. What each one does, and what decides how much it helps your particular property:

| Measure | Why it can affect an EPC | What determines the impact |
|---|---|---|
| LED lighting | The proportion of fixed low-energy fittings is recorded | How many fittings are already low-energy |
| Hot water cylinder jacket | Cylinder insulation type and thickness are recorded | Whether the cylinder is bare, thinly insulated or already foam-insulated |
| Heating controls | Controls affect calculated heating efficiency | Which of thermostat, programmer and TRVs already exist |
| Draught proofing | Reduces uncontrolled heat loss | Existing air-tightness and where the gaps are |
| Loft insulation top-up | Reduces heat loss through the roof | Existing depth, roof construction, whether it can be inspected |
| Cavity wall insulation | May reduce wall heat loss where walls are suitable | Wall construction, whether the cavity is already filled, geometry |
| Condensing boiler | Heating efficiency is a major input | The existing system, its age and type, and the fuel |
| Secondary glazing | Improves modelled window performance | Existing glazing, and how much of the property is covered |

## The honest caveats

Before you build a spreadsheet from the table above, four things to keep in mind.

**Gains depend on your starting point.** The same measure can matter a great deal in an uninsulated 1930s semi and barely register in a 1990s flat. The lower your starting rating, the bigger the gains tend to be, because the model rewards fixing the worst problems first.

**Points are not simply additive.** RdSAP models the whole dwelling, so measures interact. Insulating the walls reduces heating demand, which in turn shrinks the benefit of a more efficient boiler. Two measures will usually deliver less together than they would apart. If you need to hit a specific band, ask an assessor to model the combination rather than adding up estimates.

**Free funding may be available.** ECO4 funds insulation and heating measures for eligible lower-income and vulnerable households, and the Great British Insulation Scheme covers single insulation measures for homes in lower council tax bands with poorer ratings. Check eligibility before paying privately, because the same loft top-up or cavity fill might cost you nothing.

**You need a fresh EPC afterwards.** Improvements only count once a new assessment is lodged on the register. Budget for a new EPC at the end of the project to evidence the rating you have paid for.

## Where to start

Start with the certificate, not the chequebook. Your current EPC's recommendations section, or a fresh assessment if the certificate is old, tells you which of these measures apply to your property and roughly what each is worth. Then work up the budget tiers in order: the under-£500 measures first, insulation second, heating last.
