Landlords in England and Wales must have a valid EPC rated E or above for every rental property. This rule applies to all tenancies since April 2020. The proposed upgrade to band C — 2028 for new tenancies, 2030 for all — makes proactive planning essential. Fines reach £30,000 per property, and London enforcement is active.

## Current legal requirements at a glance

| Requirement | Detail |
|-------------|--------|
| Minimum EPC band | E (SAP 39+) |
| In force since | April 2020 (all tenancies) |
| Maximum fine | £30,000 per property |
| EPC validity | 10 years from issue |
| Exemptions register | PRS Exemptions Register (gov.uk) |
| Proposed new minimum | C (SAP 69+) by 2028–2030 |

The obligation attaches to the **act of letting** — granting, renewing, or continuing a tenancy. There is no retrospective application, but a statutory periodic tenancy (rolling over after a fixed term) is treated as a continuation of an existing tenancy for MEES purposes.

## Step 1: Know your portfolio's current ratings

The first action for any landlord is to confirm the current EPC rating and validity date for every property. Check the government EPC register at find-energy-certificate.service.gov.uk — search by postcode and filter by address.

Flags that require immediate action:
- **Rating F or G:** The property cannot legally be let. You are currently in breach if a tenancy is active.
- **Rating E with expiring certificate:** Renew before the certificate expires — you need a valid EPC to demonstrate compliance.
- **No EPC on register:** The property has never been assessed (common for pre-2008 properties). Commission an assessment before next letting.
- **Rating D or lower with proposed C upgrade in mind:** Start planning improvements now.

## Step 2: Understand your property types

The cost and feasibility of reaching C varies enormously by construction type. Categorise your portfolio:

### Cavity-wall properties (1920–1990 construction)

Found across outer-London in Ealing, Croydon, Bromley, Enfield, Barnet, Sutton, Havering, and Bexley. These are the most straightforward to improve:

- Cavity wall insulation: £400–£800, adds 5–10 SAP points
- Loft insulation to 270mm: £400–£600, adds 4–8 SAP points
- LED lighting: £80–£150, adds 1–4 SAP points
- Total to reach C from D: typically £800–£2,000

Many of these properties qualify for ECO4 or GBIS funding — potentially free. Prioritise these properties for improvement now.

### Victorian and Edwardian solid-wall properties (pre-1919)

The dominant type in inner London — Hackney, Islington, Lambeth, Wandsworth, Southwark, Tower Hamlets, Newham, and the inner parts of most other boroughs.

These require more significant intervention to reach C:
- External wall insulation: £12,000–£18,000 (highest impact, requires planning/conservation area check)
- Internal wall insulation: £8,000–£14,000 (loses floor area, disrupts interior)
- Solar PV: £5,000–£9,000 (often the most cost-effective single measure)
- Combination of cheap measures (LED + loft + heating controls): may reach C from high D without wall works

Assess your exact SAP score first. A Victorian terrace at D66 may reach C with LEDs and loft insulation. One at D55 almost certainly needs either wall insulation or solar PV.

### Post-war flats and maisonettes (1945–1980)

Purpose-built flats in Tower Hamlets, Lambeth, Southwark, Newham, Hackney, and Brent. Heating system is often the critical factor:
- Electric storage heaters: typically D or E — improving the heating system is the main lever
- Communal gas (via heat network): often C already
- Individual gas boiler: usually D, achievable to C with insulation measures

For flats with storage heaters, replacing with a gas boiler (if feasible) or modern electric heat pump heating improves the rating significantly but involves building consent and may require freeholder involvement.

## Step 3: Prioritise improvements strategically

Not all improvements make equal sense for a landlord. Priorities:

**Act first on F/G properties** — these are actively non-compliant. Improvement is non-optional.

**Act next on E properties approaching the proposed C deadline** — D-to-C is a known requirement by 2028. If a tenancy ends and you need to re-let, you'd need C to do so under the proposed rules. Improving between tenancies avoids void-period constraints.

**Use ECO4 funding wherever possible** — the scheme ends (or transitions) in 2026. Eligible properties should be processed now. An ECO4 application for one property takes 2–4 weeks from initial contact to installation.

**Batch improvements by property type** — if you have five cavity-wall semis in Bromley, a single contractor can typically survey and install all five in a week. Economies of scale apply.

## Step 4: Exemptions — when and how to use them

Exemptions are valid only when properly registered and evidenced. They are not a permanent way out — they lapse after five years.

The most commonly applicable exemption for landlords is the **cost cap exemption**: all relevant improvements would cost more than £3,500 (or £15,000 under the proposed C standard).

To register correctly:
1. Get quotes from at least three registered installers for each recommended improvement
2. Carry out any improvements that collectively cost under the cap (you cannot claim an exemption without exhausting the sub-cap measures first)
3. Submit the exemption form on the PRS Exemptions Register with evidence (quotes, completion certificates)

Common mistakes that invalidate exemptions:
- Getting only one quote
- Not carrying out the sub-cap measures before claiming the exemption
- Letting the five-year exemption expire without re-registering

## Step 5: The proposed C standard — planning now

Even without legislation, the direction is clear. The most useful thing a landlord can do in 2026 is understand which properties will be most expensive to improve and factor this into:
- Purchase decisions (buying a Victorian terrace with an F rating requires an improvement budget)
- Remortgage planning (some lenders now offer preferential rates for C-rated properties)
- Tenancy planning (improving between tenancies is far more efficient than with a tenant in situ)
